# Eloquence

Make the intended message more memorable through deliberate wording, rhythm, structure, and imagery.
Preserve meaning, evidence, and the writer's voice.

## Apply the skill

1. Identify the message, audience, medium, voice, and requested strength of treatment.
2. For a new draft, establish the message before choosing effects.
3. For revisions, find the claims, images, and transitions that deserve emphasis.
4. Choose techniques that serve those moments. Adapt their structures to the subject.
5. Check cadence, stress, and word order against natural spoken English.
6. Remove effects that weaken meaning, accuracy, pace, or voice.
7. Return the requested prose. Explain technique choices when requested or useful.

Infer preferences from the request and draft when possible.
Ask only when a missing choice materially changes the result.
Use a light touch unless the requested genre or tone calls for stronger effects.
For a critique, identify effects and suggest changes. Apply edits only when requested.

## Editorial judgement

- Put strong effects where they support the main claim, opening, transition, or ending.
- Let repeated structures develop the thought. Each unit should add meaning or pressure.
- Choose concrete images connected to the subject.
- Let wit emerge from the wording. Avoid adding unrelated jokes.
- Combine compatible devices when they strengthen the same effect.
- Keep useful plain sentences. Constant ornament removes contrast and emphasis.
- Change syntax deliberately. Awkwardness alone does not create style.
- Preserve qualifications, uncertainty, negation, chronology, and causal relationships.
- Never invent facts, numbers, evidence, quotations, or attributions for an effect.
- Keep sample wording as illustration. Borrow the pattern and write for the current subject.
- Respect the user's grammar, length, tone, and formatting constraints.
- Keep procedural wording and factual claims precise.

The references cover all 39 chapters of Mark Forsyth's *The Elements of Eloquence*.
They also include related terms needed to distinguish similar techniques.
Chapter numbers preserve the book's order within the thematic files.
The source material includes the user's supplied highlights.

Attributed examples are quotations. Common expressions are labelled.
Unattributed sentence examples are original illustrations.
Terminology notes identify definitions that vary across rhetorical traditions.
