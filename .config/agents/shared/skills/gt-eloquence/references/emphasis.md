# Emphasis

Use this reference for contrast, restraint, surprise, intensity, and accumulated detail.
Chapter numbers refer to *The Elements of Eloquence*.
Example conventions are defined in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md#reference-conventions).

## 3. Antithesis

Place opposing ideas beside each other, often within balanced structures.
The opposition sharpens a distinction and makes both sides easier to remember.

- "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times." Dickens.
- "Man proposes, God disposes." Proverb.
- "The interface became simpler. The problem became harder."

Use a real opposition. Do not invent a false choice for a balanced sentence.

## 7. Aposiopesis

Deliberately stop before completing a sentence.
The missing words suggest emotion, threat, restraint, or shared knowledge.

- "If you touch that switch again..."
- "I thought you were... Never mind."
- "When in Rome..." Familiar wording lets readers supply the ending.

An ellipsis alone does not establish the figure.
The interruption must leave something meaningful unsaid.
Do not omit information that the reader needs to act correctly.

## 23. Paradox

State an apparent contradiction that reveals a coherent insight.
The wording makes readers pause. The underlying idea must reward that pause.

- "Less is more." Common aphorism.
- "The only constant is change." Common aphorism.
- "To stay in control, learn to let go."

Check that the statement has an intelligible interpretation.
A contradiction alone does not guarantee insight.

## 26. The Fourteenth Rule

Use a particular number where a general quantity would suffice.
The precision can make an image feel concrete, strange, memorable, or comic.

- "She counted the forty-seven cracks above her bed."
- "He arrived at seventeen minutes past midnight."
- "I have exactly nine reasons to leave."

This is Forsyth's chapter label, rather than a standard classical term.
The [chapter summary confirms the numerical focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Elements_of_Eloquence#26:_The_Fourteenth_Rule).
In factual writing, use supported numbers. Specificity must preserve accuracy.

## 28. Litotes

Affirm something by denying its opposite.
This often produces understatement, restraint, or dry approval.

- "Not bad." Common expression.
- "She is no fool."
- "This is no small task."

Its strength depends on context. "Not bad" can mean acceptable or excellent.
Keep explicit limits and important judgements clear.

## 31. Pleonasm

Repeat meaning through words that add no essential information.
Deliberate redundancy can add insistence, rhythm, or emotional weight.

- "I saw it with my own two eyes." Common expression.
- "Free, gratis, and for nothing." Common expression.
- "The room was empty, utterly and completely empty."

Remove redundancy that adds neither meaning nor force.
Do not confuse emphatic repetition with an accidentally padded sentence.

## 34. Hyperbole

Exaggerate deliberately to express intensity.
Readers recognise the departure from literal measurement and understand the emotional scale.

- "I've told you a thousand times." Common expression.
- "The queue stretched halfway to the moon."
- "My inbox could bury a city."

Use it for humour, frustration, wonder, or emphasis.
Keep exaggeration recognisable. Do not disguise it as evidence or a factual promise.

## 35. Adynaton

Express impossibility, often through an impossible condition or comparison.
This is a particularly extreme form of hyperbole.
Use it for refusal, certainty, or an unattainable demand.

- "When pigs fly." Common expression.
- "I'll agree when the sea turns to stone."
- "You will sooner teach a stone to sing."

Some traditions include declarations that something exceeds all possible expression.
See [adynaton](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/A/adynaton.htm).

## 37. Congeries

Accumulate related words or phrases into a heap.
The list can amplify, overwhelm, describe, or build momentum.

- "Dust, rust, cracked glass, torn curtains, dead flies."
- "We need a plan, a budget, a deadline, an owner."
- "The room was narrow, airless, gloomy, cold, and silent."

Order the items deliberately. Each should add detail or increase pressure.
Keep lists short enough for the intended effect and format.

## Related: oxymoron

Bring contradictory terms together inside a short phrase.
A paradox usually develops a larger proposition.

- "Sweet sorrow." Shakespeare, *Romeo and Juliet*.
- "Deafening silence." Common expression.

The contradictory terms should produce a recognisable impression.
