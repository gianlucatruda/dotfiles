# Imagery

Use this reference for vivid description, figurative connections, portraits, and verbal wit.
Chapter numbers refer to *The Elements of Eloquence*.
Example conventions are defined in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md#reference-conventions).

## 4. Merism

Name parts or extremes to represent a whole.
The reader supplies the complete range. This suggests completeness without requiring a full inventory.

- "We searched high and low." Common expression meaning everywhere.
- "Young and old gathered outside." Both extremes imply the whole community.
- "We checked every layer, from screen to server."

Choose parts that imply the intended whole.
Do not imply complete coverage when the underlying claim is limited.

## 5. Blazon

Describe someone through a catalogue of physical features, often using comparisons.
Traditional blazons praise a beloved person's beauty.
Each feature contributes to the complete portrait.

- "Her eyes were stars, her lips coral, her hair spun gold."
- "His hands were maps, his shoulders stone, his beard winter."
- "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun." Shakespeare, Sonnet 130.

The third example opens a *counterblazon*. It rejects conventional praise and familiar comparisons.
Choose details that reveal the person. Avoid a generic inventory of body parts.
See [blazon and counterblazon](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/education/glossary/blazon).

## 6. Synaesthesia

Describe one sense through the language of another.
The blend makes a sensation vivid or strange.
*Synesthesia* is the American spelling.

- "A silky voice." Common expression connecting touch and hearing.
- "A loud colour." Common expression connecting hearing and sight.
- "The trumpet poured a scarlet note."

The sensory connection should serve the scene or emotion.

## 14. Hendiadys

Express one idea through two nouns joined by "and".
Normally, one noun would qualify the other.
Giving both equal grammatical status adds weight and expands the impression.

- "Sound and fury." Shakespeare, *Macbeth*. Read as furious sound.
- "He came despite the rain and weather." Read as rainy weather.
- "I admire the grace and movement of the dancer." Read as graceful movement.

The second example comes from Silva Rhetoricae.
A pair of independent ideas does not automatically create hendiadys.
Check that the intended reading combines the nouns into one idea.
See [hendiadys](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/H/hendiadys.htm).

## 18. Syllepsis

Make one word govern multiple expressions while changing meaning between them.
Often, one use is literal and another figurative.
The switch creates wit or surprise.

- "Mr Pickwick took his hat and his leave." Dickens.
- "She lost her keys and her patience."
- "We raised the price and a few eyebrows."

Here, "took", "lost", and "raised" each perform two semantic jobs.
Keep both readings natural enough for the reader to recognise the switch.

This reference follows Forsyth's distinction between syllepsis and zeugma.
Other traditions classify syllepsis as a kind of zeugma.
See [zeugma](structure.md#22-zeugma) and [syllepsis](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/S/syllepsis.htm).

## 27. Catachresis

Force a word into an unusual or apparently improper application.
The collision creates an image that ordinary usage cannot provide.
It often overlaps with bold metaphor.

- "I will speak daggers to her." Shakespeare, *Hamlet*.
- "He drank the afternoon dry."
- "She sang the room blue."

The intended meaning must remain recoverable.
Definitions range from improper application to necessary extension where ordinary vocabulary fails.
Avoid treating every metaphor as catachresis.
See [catachresis](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/C/catachresis.htm).

## 29. Metonymy and synecdoche

### Metonymy

Represent something through a closely associated thing.
The association can involve place, institution, author, instrument, or attribute.

- "Downing Street refused to comment." The place represents government officials.
- "The crown demanded loyalty." The crown represents royal authority.
- "She prefers reading Shakespeare." The author represents his works.

The connection need not involve physical contact.
Name the actual actor when the substitution would obscure responsibility.
See [metonymy](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/M/metonymy.htm).

### Synecdoche

Represent a whole through a part, or a part through a whole.
The defining relationship is between part and whole.

- "We need more hands." Hands represent workers.
- "They counted fifty head of cattle." Heads represent whole animals.
- "He bought a new set of wheels." Wheels represent a vehicle.

Choose a part that readers can connect to the intended whole.
See [synecdoche](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/S/synecdoche.htm).

## 30. Transferred epithets

Move a description from its logical owner to a nearby noun.
The transfer colours the scene with someone's condition or state of mind.

- "She endured a sleepless night." The person cannot sleep.
- "He smoked a nervous cigarette." The smoker feels nervous.
- "We followed him down the anxious corridor." The people feel anxious.

A common technical name is *hypallage*. That term also has broader historical uses.
Keep the real owner of the description apparent.
See [hypallage](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/H/hypallage.htm).

## 33. Personification

Give human qualities, actions, or agency to nonhuman things.
This makes abstractions and surroundings behave like characters.

- "Death politely held the door."
- "The city woke and rubbed its eyes."
- "Monday dragged a chair beside my bed."

An extended personification can support an allegory.
Distinguish a human action from an institutional substitution.
"The office called" usually means its staff called. That is metonymy.
