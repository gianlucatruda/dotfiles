# Questions

Use this reference for audience involvement, deliberation, rebuke, and anticipated objections.
Chapter numbers refer to *The Elements of Eloquence*.
Example conventions are defined in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md#reference-conventions).

## 13. Rhetorical questions

Traditional names overlap. Identify what the question does before assigning its name.
Choose questions that advance the prose. Do not add them as automatic section openings.

### Erotesis

Ask a question that implies an assertion.
*Erotema* is another common name.

- "Can we call this justice?"
- "Who could forget that night?"

Use it when the implied answer carries the argument.
Check that the intended audience can reasonably share that answer.

### Hypophora

Ask and then answer the speaker's own question.
This directs attention and controls the explanation.
*Anthypophora* is often used for the same pattern.
Some traditions distinguish the question from its answer.

- "What changed? Everything."
- "Why start here? Because this is where customers leave."

Use it when the answer is worth the interruption.
Avoid a repeated question-and-answer template throughout an ordinary explanation.
See [anthypophora](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/A/anthypophora.htm).

### Anacoenosis

Invite the audience's judgement, often through shared interests.
This makes the audience participate in reaching the conclusion.

- "Would you accept these conditions for your own family?"
- "Which would you choose: another promise or a working service?"

The question can invite actual participation or imply an expected judgement.
Do not claim agreement where the audience's interests genuinely differ.
See [anacoenosis](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/A/anacoenosis.htm).

### Epiplexis

Question someone to reproach, rebuke, or express grief.
The purpose is emotional pressure or judgement.

- "How many warnings must you ignore?"
- "Have you no shame?"

Choose it only when the requested voice supports confrontation or lament.
See [epiplexis](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/E/epiplexis.htm).

### Aporia

Express uncertainty or deliberation through a question or statement of doubt.
The doubt can be sincere or performed.
This lets readers witness a difficult judgement.

- "Where do we begin after such a loss?"
- "Can I defend him without betraying her?"

Make the dilemma specific. Avoid empty hesitation that adds no insight.
See [aporia](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/A/aporia.htm).

### Pysma

Ask several successive questions that together require a complex reply.
This creates pressure, urgency, or investigative momentum.

- "Who knew? Who agreed? Who benefited? Who paid?"
- "What failed? When? Where? Under whose authority?"

Each question should expose a distinct part of the issue.
See [pysma](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/P/pysma.htm).

### Procatalepsis

Anticipate an objection and answer it.
This related technique can use questions, but questions are optional.

- "You may call this expensive. Delay costs more."
- "Perhaps the tool seems complex. The default setup needs two clicks."

Address a plausible objection fairly. Do not invent a weaker opposing position.
Keep the answer supported by the supplied facts.

Some traditions call this *prolepsis*.
Forsyth's pronoun technique uses that name differently. See [prolepsis and cataphora](structure.md#36-prolepsis).
See [procatalepsis](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/P/procatalepsis.htm).
