# Repetition

Use this reference for recurring words, verbal links, refrains, and circular structures.
Chapter numbers refer to *The Elements of Eloquence*.
Example conventions are defined in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md#reference-conventions).

## Pattern guide

The same passage can contain several devices.

| Technique | Pattern | Defining feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Polyptoton | root, related form | Changed grammatical form or function |
| Anadiplosis | `... A / A ...` | One ending becomes the next beginning |
| Diacope | `A ... A` | Repetition after a brief interruption |
| Epistrophe | `... A / ... A` | Repeated endings |
| Epizeuxis | `A A A` | Immediate repetition |
| Epanalepsis | `A ... A` | Matching opening and closing boundaries |
| Anaphora | `A ... / A ...` | Repeated beginnings |
| Antanaclasis | same word, changed sense | Meaning changes between uses |
| Symploce | `A ... B / A ... B` | Repeated beginnings and endings |

## 2. Polyptoton

Repeat a word root through different grammatical forms.
Forsyth includes inflections, related derivations, and changes in grammatical function.
Use it to connect an action, its actor, or its result.

- "Judge not, that ye be not judged." Matthew 7:1, King James Bible.
- "Nothing succeeds like success." Proverb.
- "Piper, pipe that song again." William Blake, *Introduction*.

Preserve the grammatical relationship. Repeating an unchanged word alone does not establish polyptoton.

## 9. Anadiplosis

Repeat an ending at the beginning of the next clause or sentence.
The linked wording builds a ladder of ideas.

- "Fear leads to anger. Anger leads to hate." Yoda, *The Phantom Menace*.
- "We need trust. Trust requires evidence."
- "The draft became a plan. The plan became a promise."

A verbal chain does not prove a causal connection.
Each step must remain valid when its repeated wording is removed.

## 12. Diacope

Repeat a word or phrase after a brief interruption.
The interruption can add a name, qualification, or emotional appeal.

- "Bond, James Bond." James Bond.
- "Wait, my friend, wait."
- "A horse! A horse! My kingdom for a horse!" Shakespeare, *Richard III*.

The third example extends the basic pattern to `A A ... A`.
Give the interruption a purpose. A random inserted word adds little.

## 15. Epistrophe

Repeat words at the ends of successive clauses, sentences, or lines.
The repeated ending becomes the destination of each thought.
*Epiphora* is another common name.

- "We argued for change. We voted for change. We waited for change."
- "The brief demanded speed. The budget demanded speed. The launch demanded speed."
- "They sold hope. We bought hope."

Choose an ending worth remembering. Let each preceding unit develop the thought.

## 17. Epizeuxis

Repeat a word or phrase immediately, without intervening words.
Use it for concentrated urgency, grief, insistence, or excitement.

- "Never, never, never, never, never!" Shakespeare, *King Lear*.
- "Now, now, now."
- "Gone. Gone."

Frequent use weakens the force. Reserve it for moments that merit concentration.

## 32. Epanalepsis

Repeat the opening word or phrase at the end of a unit.
The unit can be a clause, sentence, or larger passage.
Use it for a frame, a return, or a sense of entrapment.

- "Nothing will come of nothing." Shakespeare, *King Lear*.
- "Home was all she wanted, home."
- "War breeds more war."

The ending should gain meaning from what happened between the repetitions.

## 39. Anaphora

Repeat opening words across successive clauses, sentences, or lines.
Use it to build rhythm, momentum, and unity across distinct claims.

- "We need time. We need trust. We need proof."
- "Let the facts speak. Let the figures stand. Let the reader decide."
- "Here we waited. Here we failed. Here we began again."

Each repeated opening should introduce a meaningful development.
The repeated phrase can otherwise overshadow everything that follows.

## Related: antanaclasis

Repeat the same word while changing its meaning.
Polyptoton changes grammatical form or function. Antanaclasis changes sense.
An example can satisfy both descriptions.

- "We broke the record, then broke for lunch."
- "We charge for repairs. The batteries charge for free."

The two meanings should be clear enough to create recognition, rather than confusion.
See [antanaclasis](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/A/antanaclasis.htm).

## Related: symploce

Combine anaphora and epistrophe.
Repeat both the beginning and ending across successive units.

- "When we win, we learn. When we lose, we learn."
- "In peace, we serve. In crisis, we serve."

The changing middle must justify the repeated frame.
See [symploce](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/S/symploce.htm).
