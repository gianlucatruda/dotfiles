# Structure

Use this reference for word order, clause relationships, balance, suspense, and compression.
Chapter numbers refer to *The Elements of Eloquence*.
Example conventions are defined in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md#reference-conventions).

## 8. Hyperbaton

Depart from normal word order to give words unusual emphasis.
The change can create grandeur, surprise, rhythm, or deliberate awkwardness.

- "Stone walls do not a prison make." Richard Lovelace, *To Althea, from Prison*.
- "Dark was the room."
- "This much, at least, we know."

Keep the sentence immediately understandable. Inversion must earn its disruption.

## 10. Periodic sentences

Delay grammatical completion until the final clause or phrase.
The opening creates an expectation that the ending resolves.
Use the delay for suspense and strong final emphasis.

- "When the noise stopped, we heard the answer."
- "After three meetings, two rewrites, and one sleepless night, she said yes."
- "If the terms are clear and the evidence holds, we can proceed."

Length alone does not make a sentence periodic. Delayed completion is the defining feature.
Keep the opening manageable enough for readers to retain.
See [periodic sentences](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/P/period.htm).

## 11. Hypotaxis, parataxis, polysyndeton, and asyndeton

The first pair concerns relationships between clauses.
The second pair concerns conjunctions. These choices can combine.

### Hypotaxis

Use subordinate clauses to show cause, condition, concession, or another dependent relationship.

- "Because the roads flooded, the buses stopped."
- "Although the launch succeeded, we stayed because several customers still needed help."

Use it for explanation and qualification. Excessive nesting burdens readers.

### Parataxis

Place clauses beside each other without subordinating one to another.

- "The rain fell. The roads flooded. The buses stopped."
- "She opened the file, and he read the first page."

Use it for directness, pace, and equal emphasis.
Make important causal relationships explicit when the reader needs them.

### Polysyndeton

Repeat conjunctions more often than ordinary syntax requires.

- "We packed bread and cheese and apples and wine."
- "The forms and the calls and the waiting wore her down."

Use it for accumulation, persistence, exhaustion, or abundance.

### Asyndeton

Omit conjunctions between coordinated items or clauses.

- "We packed bread, cheese, apples, wine."
- "Read it, mark it, cut it, send it."

Use it for speed or sharp, separate impacts.

## 16. Tricolon

Arrange three parallel words, phrases, or clauses.
The third unit can complete, intensify, or overturn the first two.

- "I came, I saw, I conquered." Julius Caesar, in English translation.
- "Life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness." US Declaration of Independence.
- "Promise the moon. Deliver the invoice. Blame the weather."

The third example gives the final unit a comic turn.
Use three units when the content supports them. Do not manufacture an empty third item.

## 19. Isocolon

Balance successive units through matching structure and equal or closely matched length.
Strict definitions require equal length. Broader English usage accepts approximate balance.

- "Easy to learn. Hard to master." Common expression.
- "Small in size. Large in scope."
- "We came to build. We stayed to learn."

Isocolon concerns balance. Tricolon concerns three units. One passage can use both.
Do not distort a claim to make its clauses symmetrical.
See [isocolon](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/I/isocolon.htm).

## 20. Enallage

Forsyth uses this term for a deliberate departure from expected grammar.
The unusual form attracts attention or establishes a voice.

- "Think different." Apple slogan.
- "We was robbed." Common expression.
- "She don't wait for permission."

Judge the effect against the intended speaker and register.
A dialect feature is not automatically a mistake.
Use standard grammar when the requested format requires it.
Other traditions define enallage more broadly as grammatical substitution.
See [enallage](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/E/enallage.htm).

## 22. Zeugma

In Forsyth's usage, supply a word once and omit its understood repetitions.
This compresses parallel clauses and can make the second clause especially crisp.

- "The good end happily and the bad unhappily." Oscar Wilde.
- "I chose tea, she coffee."
- "One team builds bridges, another walls."

Readers recover "end", "chose", and "builds" in the shortened clauses.
The omitted words must remain easy to recover.

Some traditions use zeugma as an umbrella term that includes syllepsis.
Here, syllepsis specifically changes the governing word's meaning between its applications.
See [syllepsis](imagery.md#18-syllepsis) and the [broader definition of zeugma](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/Z/zeugma.htm).

## 24. Chiasmus

Reverse ideas or grammatical structures in an `A B / B A` arrangement.
The reversal creates symmetry and emphasis.

- "She spoke with confidence. With doubt, he listened."
- "Pleasure's a sin, and sometimes sin's a pleasure." Byron.
- "We design the system. Then the system designs us."

The first reverses grammatical arrangement. The others reverse repeated words.
The more precise name for reversing repeated words is *antimetabole*.
Forsyth commonly treats these examples under chiasmus.

A symmetrical sentence can imply a false equivalence. Preserve the argument's actual relationships.
See [chiasmus](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/C/chiasmus.htm) and [antimetabole](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/A/antimetabole.htm).

## 36. Prolepsis

Forsyth uses this term for introducing a pronoun before naming its referent.
The later noun resolves the expectation created by the pronoun.

- "When she finally spoke, Mara silenced the room."
- "Before it failed, the engine rattled for weeks."
- "They had arrived at last, the people he feared most."

The precise modern linguistic term is *cataphora*.
Avoid ambiguity when several people or objects could match the pronoun.
See the [Cambridge definition](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/dictionary/english/cataphora).

Elsewhere, prolepsis can mean anticipating objections or describing future events as already accomplished.
Use [procatalepsis](questions.md#procatalepsis) for the objection technique.
See the [other meanings of prolepsis](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/P/prolepsis.htm).

## 38. Scesis onomaton

Construct descriptions from nouns and modifiers, without a finite verb.
Use it for compressed portraits, abrupt emphasis, or immediate scene setting.

- "Space: the final frontier." *Star Trek*.
- "A wet pavement. An empty station. A solitary glove."
- "One room. Two chairs. No witness."

Keep fragments deliberate and readable.
Some references also use this name for successive synonymous expressions.
Forsyth's chapter focuses on descriptions without main verbs.
See [both definitions](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/S/scesis%20onomaton.htm).
